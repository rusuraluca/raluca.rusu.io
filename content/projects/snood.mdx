## Overview

SNOOD is a full-stack marketplace that lets restaurants, bakeries, and stores sell end-of-day surplus instead of throwing it out. I designed and built the whole platform: a React consumer app, a Node.js + PostgreSQL API, and a partner-facing dashboard. Two sides of the market in one build: customers hunting for discounted meals, and merchants who needed it to be worth their time.

## Why it mattered

Over a third of food is wasted globally, and roughly 10% of greenhouse-gas emissions come from food that never gets eaten. Restaurants account for about 40% of that waste, not because they're careless, but because daily demand is genuinely hard to predict.

A survey of 400 local students showed the demand side was ready:

- **75%** struggle to keep a balanced diet on a budget
- **97%** regularly eat reheated meals
- **60%** would happily pick up discounted orders in person

The intersection was real: waste on one side, budget pressure on the other, and a pickup model people already told us they'd use.

## What I built

**End-of-day surplus ordering.** Customers browse nearby partners, reserve discounted meals, and pick them up before closing, on mobile and web.

**B2B analytics dashboard.** Most food-saving platforms at the time just listed discounts. I gave restaurant partners monthly breakdowns of waste and order trends, so they could adjust purchasing and cooking volumes instead of guessing. The data was the differentiator.

**Tiered subscriptions.** Free, Green, and Sustainable plans gave partners a path from "try it" to "pay for deeper waste-reduction insights": a real revenue model instead of a free-for-all.

## Results

**1st place in the web section** (30 teams) at PoliHackathon 2022.

From a 48-hour prototype, SNOOD moved into **direct partnerships with local businesses and real customer orders**. The product worked: people actually used the pipeline between waste and a discounted meal.

It didn't become the long-term winner. bonapp.eco and Munch had more distribution, brand recognition, and funding, and they took the market. I'd call it a useful failure: the demand, product, and tech were validated. The go-to-market wasn't, and at hackathon scale it was never going to be.

## What I'd do differently

One city, one merchant category, one repeatable pickup flow. Sharpen the wedge before adding feature surface area or subscription tiers.
